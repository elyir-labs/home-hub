When the instrument has finished, the light can just be a light.

The bridge is not a devkit on a shelf: it is a small object on a socket in a living room, and its light is the only expressive surface it has. Placing spends that light on one question — “is here good?” — and the question is answered within a minute of plugging it in. What it does for the other fourteen hours is a decision nobody has taken. Taking it: once it is home, and only if you say so, the light becomes a nightlight — and gives itself straight back the moment anything is wrong.

Why it has to be able to stop

Green is a promise: mesh and broker, both up. A glow that carries on while the bridge is down is furniture that lies, and nobody checks furniture. So a fault takes the light back — and that is the feature, not the price. A nightlight that has turned amber is the most legible fault report in the house, read at 2am by somebody who was only walking past.

It never outranks the radio

The real risk is that people start choosing sockets for where a nightlight looks good rather than where the mesh needs one. Precedence settles it without a word: move it somewhere thin and it stops being a nightlight and goes back to breathing red. The placement instrument stays in charge of its own argument.

What it asks of the board

Three things the product board owes it, each the fix for something the devkit got wrong. The LED lit however the thing is plugged in — on the clone S3 only the UART-side port feeds its rail. A data line that is actually driven, so a slow fade stops being the pale-green flicker bug and becomes a sunset ramp. And an emitter the enclosure can diffuse: one pixel behind a pinhole is a maker’s nightlight, not a thing you want in a living room.
