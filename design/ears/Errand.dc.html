A — the puck runs the errand

The hub does not need to hear the strip. It needs something in that room to do as it is told.

A bridge puck is already in a socket in the middle of the house, already has a Bluetooth radio, and is already holding an MQTT session to the hub. So the hub stops trying to reach the strip and hands the puck an errand instead — and the conversation it hands over is sealed: the door the strip opens is opened by the hub and closed by the strip, and the puck is the corridor between them.

The hub In the garage, where the cable is. Opens the SRP6a session, holds the household’s Wi‑Fi, decides what a strip is told. Its own radio is never used for this and need not exist.
MQTT strip/errand
The bridge puck In a hallway socket, because that is where it has to be to reach the wall switches. It scans, connects, writes what it was given and reports what came back. It is a courier and knows nothing.

The puck carries the bytes and cannot read one of them. A proxy that cannot decrypt adds no trust surface — it can drop an errand, and that is the whole of what it can do.

Nothing on the wall changes. Not a word, not a beat — and no screen ever says “puck”.
For

It is the only answer that costs the household nothing. No chooser, no second app, no tap, no new object — a house with our bridge in it already has the hardware. It frees the hub’s own radio entirely, which matters on a mini PC that has none, and it scales the obvious way: more of our things in the house, more ears.

Against

It needs a puck, and the first thing a house adopts may be the strip. Then there is no ear and this answer is not available — which is the board on the far right. The radio risk is settled, and not the way this box used to fear it: a puck held the second link for 61 s with the mesh up and a switch still obeying (item 38). What is left is a price. The mesh runs at a fifth of its rate while an errand lasts, and a scan stops it dead — so an errand must end, and the hub must send an address rather than a search.
